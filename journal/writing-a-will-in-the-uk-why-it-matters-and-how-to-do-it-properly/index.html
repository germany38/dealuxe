Writing a Will in the UK: Why It Matters and How to Do It Properly

Few people enjoy thinking about death, but a will is one of the most useful documents you can create for the people you love. Without one, the law decides who inherits, and the result may not match your wishes. This article is general information, not legal advice.

Why have a will?

A will lets you decide who receives your property, who looks after dependent children and who manages your affairs. It can reduce uncertainty and conflict for your family. It matters especially if you have children, a partner you are not married to, property, savings or a business.

What a will usually contains

  1. Executors: the people who carry out your wishes.
  2. Beneficiaries: who receives what.
  3. Guardians: who will care for minor children.
  4. Specific gifts: items or sums for particular people or charities.
  5. The residue: what remains after specific gifts.
  6. Funeral wishes, if you wish to record them.

Choosing executors and guardians

Pick people you trust, who are organised and willing. Talk to them first, and consider a back-up in case someone cannot act. For guardians, think about values, location and the ability to care for your children.

Make a list of your assets

Note property, bank accounts, pensions, investments, valuable items and digital assets. Keep the list updated, and check how assets such as jointly held property or pensions pass on, because some do not follow the will.

Simple wills versus complex situations

A simple will suits straightforward circumstances. If you have a blended family, overseas property, a business, a disabled beneficiary or complex tax issues, consider professional advice from a specialist.

Making it legally valid

In England and Wales, a will generally must be in writing, signed by you and witnessed by two people who are present at the same time and are not beneficiaries. Rules differ in Scotland and Northern Ireland. A professional can make sure the formalities are met.

Keep it safe and up to date

Store the original in a safe place and tell your executors where it is. Review your will after life events such as marriage, divorce, a new child or a major change in assets.

Common mistakes

Not making a will. Using vague wording. Choosing witnesses who are beneficiaries. Forgetting to update. Not telling executors where the will is.

FAQ

How long does it take? It depends on complexity, so ask about timescales. Can I change it later? Yes, usually by a codicil or a new will. Do I need a solicitor? Not always, but advice helps in complex cases.

Conclusion

A will is a simple act of care. Make a list of assets, choose your people and review it regularly. If you would like a guided service, you can see what Crystal Life Wills offers and compare it with other providers.
